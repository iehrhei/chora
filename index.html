Chora is an old Greek word for place—not a point on a map, but the room in which something is allowed to happen. The Greeks also used it for the farmland beyond a city’s walls: outside, and still belonging. In the Timaeus, Plato reached for it when he needed a third thing, something that holds every appearance and becomes none of them, “the receptacle, and in a manner the nurse, of all generation.” He admitted that we glimpse it only sideways, as in a dream. Julia Kristeva later took the word for the place where a voice is held before it learns to speak.

This wall is a chora of the smallest kind. It hangs in a workroom called the Ouvroir de Rhei, among a book of fungi, Haeckel’s lichens, a plant leaning toward the window—and an ordinary mirror on a leather strap. Look into it and it will show you yourself, as mirrors do.

Wait.

Somewhere a camera rests on the seafloor, or a robot is descending with its lamps on, and the mirror may let the deep sea through.

Nothing here can break. Move what you like; leave a word in pencil, and it will stay on your wall alone. To the room where things are allowed to happen, this wall is dedicated.
